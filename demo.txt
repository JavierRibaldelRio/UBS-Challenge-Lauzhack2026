D. QUESTIONS JUDGES WILL PROBABLY ASK (short answers)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Q: Is this really a "twin", or just a calculator?
A: It starts from this person's data and learned habits (24 months of bank
   history), and it stays alive: you can tell it what changed today ("I now earn
   1,500") and it updates itself; you can change any path in a follow-up and
   undo it; it answers questions with a confidence it computes, explains the
   trade-offs, and marks clearly what it does not know (dotted lines, "?").

Q: What kinds of variables does the model have?
A: Two, exactly as UBS defines a twin (decisions vs external conditions).
   IDIOSYNCRATIC = the person: their income, rent, spending habit, their
   decisions (each path), and their personal luck (surprise expenses,
   bonuses), learned from their own 24 months of bank history.
   MACROECONOMIC = the economy: inflation vs pay, interest rates, markets,
   recessions, house prices. One setting for EVERY path (the "The economy"
   bar, presets = UBS scenarios S101-S110), so decisions are compared under
   the same conditions. In one message the chat splits them: "master's, and a
   recession in 2 years" -> the master's is a path, the recession is the economy.

Q: Does it meet UBS's brief?
A: All four MUSTs: fictional profile, working scenarios, before/after
   comparison, transparent assumptions. Both BONUSes: a conversational AI,
   and sensitivity & uncertainty. And the reporting template: "scenario
   report (UBS template)" exports the 15 fields UBS lists.

Q: Is the AI smart enough to follow a conversation?
A: Yes, because it gets the context: the last messages, the person's current
   numbers, and every path on screen with its results. So "have the kid at 35
   instead" edits the right path, "what's my rent?" gets the real number, and
   "no, I meant rent, not saving" removes the wrong path and fixes it.
   (A first version sent each message alone, and it was clearly worse.
   The model was fine; it just didn't have the context.)

Q: What happens if I ask for something impossible (e.g. a 200k down payment
   without the money)?
A: It doesn't hide it: the tile says "can't pay the down payment", the chart
   shows cash going below zero, and "can I afford it?" is answered "No".

Q: Is this a prediction?
A: No. It is a simulation under assumptions you can see and change. It shows
   possible futures, not the future. The shaded band shows normal month-to-
   month ups and downs, learned from the data.

Q: Which assumptions affect the result most?
A: The Sensitivity card (under the charts). Left: what moves the result
   most (each input ±10%), for A and B. Right: pick an input and see both paths
   from −30% to +30%, and where their order flips. For the master's, income matters most (±83k for
   a 10% change), then other spending (±15k) and rent (±11k). The biggest
   unknown is the degree's salary premium; that's why we show the break-even
   (24%).

Q: How does a decision change the financial state?
A: A decision is a list of life events (job, study, move, home...). The app
   turns it into a month-by-month plan: income, rent, costs, debt, home value.
   Then exact accounting runs every month: new cash = old cash + money in −
   money out.

Q: How did you verify the calculations?
A: Four ways. 64 automated tests (plus 54 tests against the real AI), including UBS's own validation list (zero
   income, higher tuition, loan payoff, transfers). Live checks on screen
   (cash adds up, net worth = assets − debts, debt never negative, same seed =
   same result). A backtest on real 2026 data. And we checked UBS's ledger
   itself: zero accounting errors in 28,800 rows.

Q: What happens if a constraint is broken, for example cash goes negative?
A: We never hide it. The tile says "cash runs out at 20", the node turns red, and we show in how
   many of the 500 futures it happens and how low it goes. This matches the
   data's own "requires financing" flag. We don't secretly borrow money.

Q: Where is the AI? Where is the machine learning?
A: Three places. 1) Machine learning on 28,800 months of data: seasonal
   spending (February and August are expensive), the December bonus (54% of
   employed people, about 61% of a month's pay), the real pattern of surprise
   expenses, each person's normal spending, and how spending changes when life
   changes. 2) Monte Carlo uses that to simulate 500 futures per path.
   3) An LLM turns words into events, and numbers into words.

Q: Why didn't you train a model to predict outcomes directly?
A: Nobody in the data does a master's or moves abroad, so no model can learn
   "what if" from it. It would only repeat averages and break the accounting.
   So we learned how months behave, and kept the money maths exact.

Q: How is the "confidence" computed? Is it real, or did the AI make it up?
A: The AI never decides it. The simulator computes it from three checks.
   1) Luck: in how many of 500 futures, with the same luck for both paths,
      does one path end higher? We tested this kind of statement on almost
      20,000 pairs of real people in the data. When we said "at least 95%",
      it was true 99.9% of the time. When we said 66%, it was true 67%.
   2) Peer range: does the result still hold if her salary is at the low end
      of real people her age in the data, and the other path's at the high end?
   3) Unknowns: does it depend on a number nobody knows? If so, how much
      would have to change to flip it?
   "Can I reach my goal?" questions are answered by the chance of reaching it.
   0% or 100% is clear-cut; around 50% is low confidence.

Q: The docs say Monte Carlo is not a digital twin. Why do you use it?
A: We agree. Monte Carlo only handles normal month-to-month luck, and we
   checked against real data that it does that honestly. What really decides
   "master's or work" is the assumptions. For that we use what the docs
   suggest: sensitivity ("what matters most"), break-even (a small
   optimization: "the master's needs +24% salary"), robustness against real
   peer ranges, and clearly marked unknowns. Decisions are applied to an
   explicit model of income, costs and debt (a causal "do" on the plan), not
   learned from correlations.

Q: Buying a home doesn't drop the money?
A: It drops CASH, not net worth. At the purchase, cash falls by the down
   payment (20% of the price), but that money became part of a home, so net
   worth stays flat. That is why there are two charts side by side, net worth
   and cash in the bank, and the 🏠 marks the month. We also check, in every future, that the down payment can
   really be paid from cash. If not, the tile says "can't pay the down
   payment" and "Can I afford it?" is answered "No".

Q: What are the orange 🎯 markers?
A: The person's own goals from UBS's data, for example "emergency savings,
   CHF 12,800 by December 2029" (high priority). Each marker sits at that amount
   and date on the cash chart. Below the charts, each goal shows in what % of
   futures A and B reach it, and the comparison table has a row per goal.
   A goal counts as reached if cash plus investments are above it at that date.

Q: Does it understand complicated sentences or other languages?
A: Yes. We tested 9 hard ones: two alternative lives with 9 events,
   a loan to pay for a master's, "70% unemployment benefits", an inheritance
   half invested, French, and pounds instead of francs (it refuses to convert
   them and says so). All parsed correctly, and every answer passed the
   number check.

Q: How do you stop the AI from inventing numbers?
A: Four guards. A strict JSON format. A rule: any amount the user did not say
   must be empty, and the app fills it from data with a label. The explanation
   AI only sees our computed facts. And the app checks every number in its
   text. "See the prompt" shows exactly what the AI was told.

Q: What if the AI is down?
A: The app still works: keyword rules for questions, a template for the
   explanation, and cached answers. The buttons never need the AI.

Q: Why does "start working" end so rich (745k)?
A: That comes from UBS's synthetic data: employed people earn about 8,000
   net a month at every age, and spending barely rises with income. We label
   it "from data", say it openly, and the salary can be edited in one click.

Q: Is any of this real data?
A: No. All people are fictional, from UBS's synthetic dataset. No real client
   data is used anywhere.

Q: How did you measure uncertainty, and is it honest?
A: 500 simulated futures per path with a fixed seed, and every path uses the
   same random months, so differences come from the decision, not luck. We
   tested it: trained on 2025, predicted 2026 for 1,200 people. The 8-in-10
   range caught 78.5% (ideal 80%), the middle half caught 49.8% (ideal 50%).

Q: Why is the shaded band so thin?
A: Monthly surprises average out over 10 years. Uncertainty about the big
   assumptions (salary, premium, rent) is shown separately, in "What matters
   most" and the break-even. We keep those two kinds of uncertainty apart on
   purpose.

Q: What about taxes and inflation?
A: Income in the data is already after tax. Values are in 2025 francs, so
   there is no inflation. Things we cannot model (for example new tax rules)
   are listed by the AI under "Not modelled".

Q: Isn't "fits your goals better" financial advice?
A: No, and it says so ("Not financial advice"). It is a reading of the
   simulation against the person's own goals from the data, with the rule
   shown on screen: 1) not running out of cash, 2) the goals (high priority
   counts double), 3) net worth. UBS asks that such weights are never hidden,
   and that the most money is not treated as automatically best. The app
   computes it; the AI only explains it, and its numbers are checked.

Q: Why a tree, and why only two at a time?
A: UBS's own examples describe a "scenario tree" of branching decisions, and
   real life is sequential: work, THEN maybe a flat, THEN maybe a child.
   Comparing two at a time keeps it readable: one green, one blue, one chart,
   one plain-English comparison. You can still have as many nodes as you like.

Q: What's the difference between cash and net worth?
A: Cash is money in the bank. Net worth is cash + investments + home − debts.
   For Alexia today they are the same. When she buys a flat, cash drops but
   net worth does not, because the money became a home.

Q: Can it handle several decisions at once? Other people?
A: Yes. One sentence can hold several events ("master's now, job loss at 25,
   flat at 28"). "A or B" creates separate paths, up to 3 per question.
   The dropdown has all 1,200 fictional people from the data.

Q: Which AI model do you use?
A: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on Together AI, with reasoning switched off and
   temperature 0. It answers in about 1–2 seconds. Any OpenAI-compatible
   model works; it is one line in a config file.

Q: What did you build and what did you reuse?
A: Reused: UBS's synthetic data and challenge documents, and open-source
   libraries (React, Recharts, numpy, pandas). Built during the hackathon:
   the trained model, the simulation engine, the AI gateway and the whole app.

Q: What's the impact? Who would use this?
A: Anyone facing a big life decision can test it before making it, and see
   clearly what is known, what is assumed, and what is unknown. An advisor
   could use it in a conversation with a client. It is a tool to understand
   choices, not financial advice.

Q: What would you do next?
A: Real market data for investment growth, a proper tax and mortgage module,
   more of UBS's 120 scenario ideas as buttons, and letting a person load
   their own numbers privately on their own device.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
Run once, offline (cd ml && uv run train.py, ~5 s, fixed seed = identical files
every time). It reads UBS's CSVs (1,200 people x 24 months = 28,800 rows) and
writes two files that are BUNDLED into the web app (no download at demo time):

frontend/src/model/params.json  (what was learned + the evidence)
  WHAT                          FROM THE DATA                     USED BY THE APP IN
  season[status][month]         extra spending per calendar      sim.ts: every simulated month
                                month, per job status; kept only  (students +34% in February)
                                if statistically clear (|t|>3)
  bonus[status]                 chance + size of a December       sim.ts: December of each future
                                bonus (employed: 54%, ~61% of a   (drawn at random)
                                month's pay)
  shock_quantiles (201 points)  the REAL shape of surprise         sim.ts: the random part of
                                expenses (skewed, not a bell       spending in every future
                                curve; no month-to-month memory)
  regressions.other_spending    how spending changes with income,  resolve.ts: when a decision changes
  regressions.spending_volatility rent, age, household, status     someone's situation (job, child…),
                                (CV R² 0.05 and 0.80)              spending and its ups and downs follow
  anchors (medians + 10th/90th) salary by job x age band, rent by  resolve.ts: every number the user did
                                city x household, tuition, study   not give ("median of 69 people aged
                                length                             20-24"); answer.ts: peer-range test
  backtest + calibration        trained on 2025 only, predicted    "Can you trust this?" panel; the
                                2026 for all 1,200 people          confidence rules (≥95% = high)
  data_checks                   UBS ledger: 0 identity errors,      trust panel
                                0 continuity errors
  catalog                       UBS's 120 scenario ideas           the AI tags each path with the
                                                                   closest one (catalog_id)
frontend/src/model/profiles.json  (one record per person)
  starting cash, investments, loans, income, rent, goals (with priority), study
  assumptions, the REAL 24-month bank balance (black dots on the cash chart),
  and the person's OWN learned spending level + its uncertainty (calib).

What it does NOT do: it does not predict "what if I do a master's". Nobody in
the data does a master's or moves, so that cannot be learned honestly.
Decisions are applied by exact formulas; the data teaches how normal months
behave (seasons, bonuses, surprises, habits) and the typical values.
Proof it works: trained on 2025, the "8 in 10" range caught 78.5% of real 2026
results (ideal 80), the middle half 49.8% (ideal 50); "A ends higher than B"
statements were right 99.9% of the time when we said ≥95% (19,980 pairs).


---------------------------------------------------------------------
H. WHY THIS IS A FINANCIAL TWIN, BY UBS'S OWN DEFINITION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
UBS (scenario-modelling.md §2):  x(t+1) = F( x(t), a(t), w(t); θ ),
"The model must distinguish user-controlled decisions from external conditions."
  x  the state       cash, investments, homes, debts, income, rent, spending,
                     job, household, goals: from the person's rows in the CSVs
  F  the transition  exact monthly bookkeeping (cash identity, net worth =
                     assets − debts), checked live on every change
  a  decisions       the nodes of the tree (job, study, move, home, child,
                     loan, investing…), each number labelled with its source
  w  external        the economy (inflation, rates, recession, markets, house
                     conditions   prices), ONE setting shared by every path
  θ  parameters      learned by train.py (section G)
UBS deck, "a digital twin is more than a dashboard": DATA (UBS's synthetic
ledger) -> MODEL (formulas + learned behaviour) -> SCENARIOS (the tree, A vs B)
-> EXPLAIN (plain-English tiles and comparison, every number checked).
It is a TWIN, not a calculator: it starts from this person's data and their
own learned habits, it UPDATES itself ("I now earn 1,500", "I own a 200k
flat", "never below 5k"), it keeps a memory of the conversation, and every
path recomputes from the new state.
UBS capability list (ai-and-technology.md §2): C01 state, C02 personal habits,
C03/C14 updating, C04-C07 scenarios and life events in plain words, C08
constraints (cash below zero, down payment, your own cash floor), C09 fair
comparison (same luck, same economy), C10 uncertainty + sensitivity, C11
goals, C12/C18 plain explanations, C13 interactive, C15/C19 validation and
reproducibility (fixed seed), C16/C17 every number tagged with its source,
C20 responsible: "in this simulation", "Not financial advice".

---------------------------------------------------------------------
J. WHY IT IS INNOVATIVE, AND WHY IT MATTERS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
INNOVATIVE
  - A tree of possible lives, not a list of scenarios: decisions build on each
    other (work -> then a flat -> then a child), with the time on each link,
    exactly the "scenario tree" in UBS's examples, and you can rearrange it.
  - The AI never does the maths, and is never trusted blindly: it turns words
    into a strict JSON plan; the engine computes; the AI explains; the app
    checks every number it wrote (✓ N numbers checked).
  - Confidence is COMPUTED, not claimed, and backtested on real data.
  - "Fits your goals better" is computed with the rule shown on screen (cash
    safety, then goals, then money), so more money is not automatically
    "better", as UBS's FAQ asks.
  - Unknowns are shown, not hidden: dotted lines, a break-even ("pays off only
    if salary +24%"), and a sensitivity chart.
  - A living twin: it updates itself from what you tell it and fixes its own
    mistakes (undo, remove, correct) in the conversation.
IMPACT
  - Anyone facing a big decision can rehearse it before making it, in their
    own words, and see what is known, assumed and unknown.
  - An advisor could use it live with a client: one shared picture, two paths
    side by side, plain language, no promises.
  - Built to be trusted: reproducible, validated, transparent, responsible.
